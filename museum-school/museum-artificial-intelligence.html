Why most museum AI starts in the wrong place

It starts with a tool. A vendor demos a multilingual guide, a board member reads about generative art, a grant opportunity names "AI innovation." The tool gets bought, launched in one gallery, and quietly abandoned — because nobody asked what the visitor needed at that moment, who on staff would own it, or what data it would run on.

Our work starts with the museum. Since 1999 we have planned more than forty institutions from feasibility through opening day, and the questions are the same ones we ask of any exhibit or building program: who is the visitor, what are we trying to change for them, and what does it take to operate this in year three?

"AI is a staffing, operations, and visitor-experience decision before it is a technology decision."

This engagement is the planning layer that sits underneath every AI decision — so the board, the staff, and any vendor are working from the same map.

The visitor journey: where AI helps, where people lead

A typical starting picture. Every museum is different — your map is built from your visitors, your staff, and your systems — but these are the patterns we see most often.

Stage 01Discover & plan the visit
Where AI can help
  • Answering trip-planning questions around the clock, in many languages
  • Visit suggestions by age, time available, and interests
  • School-group booking support and curriculum matching for teachers
Where people should lead
  • Accessibility and accommodation requests
  • Group and event sales, where relationships drive revenue
Stage 02Arrive & orient
Where AI can help
  • Attendance forecasting to staff the front desk and galleries
  • Wayfinding and "what's happening now" during the visit
Where people should lead
  • The welcome itself — the first greeting sets the tone for the visit
  • The moment visitors are most uncertain; kiosks don't replace staff here
Stage 03Explore & engage
Where AI can help
  • Audio description, live captions, and translation for access
  • Layered content for visitors who want to go deeper
  • Interactive exhibits where AI is the subject, not just the delivery
Where people should lead
  • Facilitated learning with children
  • Community, cultural, or sacred histories — only with community consent
  • Collection content that hasn't been verified by curators
Stage 04Café, shop & amenities
Where AI can help
  • Inventory and purchasing forecasts tied to attendance
  • Product recommendations linked to current exhibitions
Where people should lead
  • Pricing decisions — dynamic pricing can make a family visit feel unpredictable
Stage 05Depart & remember
Where AI can help
  • Analyzing open-ended survey comments and reviews at scale
  • Follow-up messages: what you saw, what to see next time
Where people should lead
  • Responding to complaints — a person should own the apology
Stage 06Return, join & give
Where AI can help
  • Identifying likely members and lapsed members from visit patterns
  • Drafting donor research, grant narratives, and reports for staff review
Where people should lead
  • Major-gift cultivation and stewardship
  • Any use of children's data or behavioral profiling without clear consent
Behind the ScenesOperations
Where AI can help
  • Collections metadata, transcription, and image tagging — with curatorial review
  • Volunteer and staff scheduling, maintenance logs, exhibit downtime tracking
  • Board packets, policy drafts, and internal knowledge search
Where people should lead
  • Curatorial voice and interpretive judgment
  • Personnel decisions, evaluations, and hiring

How the engagement works

Museum AI planning runs in five phases over twelve to sixteen weeks, from signature to board presentation.

  1. Discover (weeks 1–3). Data request, systems inventory, staff and board interviews, visitor data review, and a look at what vendors have already pitched.
  2. On-site (weeks 3–5). Three to four days walking the visit as each persona, shadowing front-line staff, and running workshops with every department.
  3. Map (weeks 5–9). Ecosystem map, service blueprint, and persona journey maps — with every AI opportunity and every people-first zone marked.
  4. Estimate (weeks 9–13). Have, buy, build, or don't pursue for each opportunity, with cost ranges, staffing needs, data readiness, and sequence.
  5. Deliver (weeks 13–16). Full report, three-to-five-page board summary, and a presentation to leadership and the board.
Key Principle
The right answer can be "buy nothing."

A vendor assessment starts with what the vendor sells. This work starts with your visitors, your mission, and your operating budget. If the honest answer is to switch on features you already pay for and invest the rest in people, the report says so.

Questions about museum AI planning

How much does museum AI planning cost?

Typically $80,000 to $130,000 exclusive of travel — $80k–$100k for a single institution and $100k–$130k for multi-site or more complex institutions. Every engagement begins with a one-hour conversation at no cost.

Do we need to be using AI already?

No. Many museums have not started, and that is often the best time — nothing has been bought yet. If you have already launched tools, we include them in the review.

Will AI replace our staff?

That is not the goal of this work. We look for places where AI takes repetitive work off staff so they can spend more time with visitors, and we mark the places where a person should always lead.

How is this different from a technology vendor's assessment?

A vendor assessment starts with what the vendor sells. Our work starts with your visitors, your mission, and your operating budget — and it can conclude that the right answer is to buy nothing.

What about visitor privacy and children's data?

Privacy, consent, and children's data are reviewed at every journey stage, and the AI use policy draft sets clear rules before any tool is purchased.

How does this connect to master planning?

The service blueprint and journey maps feed directly into a master plan or exhibition program, so AI decisions and building or gallery decisions are made together. See museum master planning.
