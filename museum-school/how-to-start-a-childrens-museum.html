Museum School

How to start a
children's museum.

Play-based institutions face the same capital and operating math as any museum — often with higher exhibit wear and staffing needs. Start with community demand, then feasibility, then capital.

Same discipline, different operating model

A children's museum is still a museum: it needs a mission, a board, a sustainable operating model, and leadership that can run programs every week — not just open a building. The difference is who you serve (families with young children), how exhibits wear (high touch, high replacement cycle), and how you staff (floor facilitation and school partnerships matter as much as the gallery).

For the full ten-step sequence — community meetings through opening day — read How to Start a Museum. This guide focuses on what is specific to children's museums.

Five steps that matter most

  1. Prove family demand. Hold community meetings with parents, school districts, pediatric networks, and parks departments. Ask what age ranges are underserved — do not lead with renderings.
  2. Visit peer institutions. Study children's museums with similar market size. Note attendance, membership pricing, exhibit replacement cycles, and staff-to-visitor ratios on the floor.
  3. Commission a feasibility study. Children's museums often assume repeat visitation that does not materialize at projected levels. A museum feasibility study tests attendance, membership, and operating costs against comparable institutions — before capital is committed.
  4. Budget exhibits for wear. Interactive children's exhibits typically run $200–350/sq ft or higher for fabrication, with ongoing refresh reserves. See how much exhibits cost for benchmarks.
  5. Hire operating leadership early. An executive director with children's museum experience should be in place before final exhibit contracts — they own safety, staffing model, and school partnerships.

Common mistakes

  • Building before validating demand — a beautiful space with insufficient annual visits is a liability, not an asset.
  • Underestimating staffing — children's museums are labor-intensive; personnel is typically 55–65% of operating budget.
  • No exhibit refresh fund — plan capital reserves for gallery rotation; static exhibits lose members.
  • Confusing preview space with permanent model — a pop-up or preview facility proves concept; it is not proof of long-term sustainability without the feasibility numbers.

Feasibility before fundraising.
