Museum School

How much do buildings
and exhibits cost?

The honest answer in 2026: reliable per-square-foot numbers — for the shell and for the galleries — are hard to give and harder to schedule against. What changed, and what boards should still plan for. Not obstacle-setting; just the world right now.

Honest answer first

Boards and donors still ask for a single number — how much per square foot? — for the building and for the exhibits, with a schedule they can put in a capital campaign. That was workable before 2020. It is much harder now.

Building cost, exhibit cost, and project schedule are all uncertain in ways they were not pre-Covid. Energy, freight, tariffs, procurement, fabrication capacity, and production tools are moving targets — some week to week. Treat any per-square-foot rule of thumb as historical context, not a budget you can fundraise against without fresh quotes tied to a real proceed date.

What used to work (pre-Covid)

Building. Depending on building type, practitioners could often place total construction cost in the ballpark of roughly $1,000/sq ft — construction and fit-out of the shell, not including land purchase (which might be high or low in any given market). You were in the ballpark enough to frame a campaign and talk to a GC.

Exhibits. For fully interactive museum galleries, the field could often cite roughly $300–$650/sq ft for exhibit fabrication and fit-out — and match that range to a fabrication calendar with reasonable confidence. Museum fabricators, installers, and traveling-exhibit producers were stable enough that you could scope a project, pick a shop, and hold a timeline.

Those norms supported the benchmarks below. They still explain what drives cost — but they no longer predict what you will pay or when work will finish.

What broke after Covid

  • Fabricator and installer capacity — many museum exhibit shops, installers, and traveling-exhibit producers did not survive the disruption. Fewer qualified bidders, longer lead times, less predictable pricing.
  • Supply chain, energy, and freight — materials and shipping costs no longer behave like pre-2020 models. Commodity and energy prices swing quickly; that flows straight into transportation and manufacturing.
  • Tariffs and trade policy — another moving layer on imported materials, equipment, and finished goods. A bid valid in one month may not survive the next.
  • Schedule risk — the same project can cost differently depending on when you lock scope and who still has crew capacity that quarter.
  • AI and production tools — software for design, visualization, fabrication prep, and content is shifting quickly — in some cases week to week — which changes how shops and contractors price labor and what they can promise on timeline.

The result is project-by-project pricing for both shell and exhibits: a number that is valid if you are ready to proceed by a specific date — with contractors, fabricators, procurement, and shipping booked — not a universal constant you can carry for months while fundraising drifts.

Historical benchmarks (pre-disruption reference)

Use this table to understand what used to be in the conversation, not as a 2026 quote. Ranges vary by interactivity, durability, geography, and whether the gallery is temporary or permanent:

New museum building (construction, not land)Roughly ~$1,000/sq ft all-in for the shell — varied by building type and market (pre-Covid ballpark)

Permanent gallery (~5,000 sq ft)Often $750k–$1.5M+ in exhibit fabrication alone
Traveling / modular exhibitionWide range — $200k–$2M+ depending on footprint and tour structure

Land is a separate line item — purchase or lease can dominate the pro forma in some cities and barely register in others. Exhibition space is often roughly half the total building program — so capital planning historically had at least three buckets: land, shell, and exhibits, each with its own logic. Today all three need fresh quotes when you have a proceed date.

What still drives cost up or down

  • Building type and systems — climate control, high bays, loading docks, and life-safety requirements for collections and crowds push shell cost well beyond generic commercial construction.
  • Interactivity — touch tables, AV, sensors, and software maintenance add first cost and ongoing operating expense.
  • Custom fabrication — one-off scenic and object mounts cost more than modular casework and graphic panels.
  • Life safety and accessibility — code compliance, fire suppression in showcases, and universal design are non-negotiable and affect budget.
  • Operating model — children's museums and science centers often budget higher per square foot because exhibits wear faster and staff facilitation is part of the experience.
  • Phasing — opening with a partial gallery and expanding in phase two spreads capital but changes donor messaging.

A dated project benchmark

On the C.O. Polk Interactive Museum (McDonough, Georgia), exhibition fabrication for roughly 1,800 sq ft was budgeted at about $590,000 — approximately $328/sq ft at high interactivity. That project predates the post-Covid disruption. It is one useful data point for what interactive civic scale felt like when the fabricator market was intact — not a number to paste into a 2026 campaign without new bids.

What boards can still do

  • Separate building from exhibits in the capital stack — they have different risk profiles and different vendor markets.
  • Test operating sustainability first — attendance, staffing, refresh reserves — before locking exhibit scope. See what a feasibility study covers.
  • Phase galleries — open with partial fit-out; expand when procurement and capacity are visible, not assumed.
  • Get quotes when you have a proceed date — for the GC and the fabricators, not twelve months earlier for numbers that will expire. Treat schedule and price as a pair.
  • Build contingency — post-disruption projects need more float on cost and calendar than pre-2020 models assumed — for shell and exhibits alike.
